Abstract

After a service visit, an HVAC contractor's assistant has to decide what to do next with each homeowner: stay quiet, check in, nudge an open estimate, ask what is blocking the decision, offer scheduling, touch a membership, park the thread, or hand off to a person. We learn that decision from 90,298 logged steps of an older policy, with no simulator and no way to test on real customers. We fit a Q-function with Fitted Q-Iteration, relabel its greedy actions with hard safety rules, and distil the result into a depth-10 decision tree that ships as a single dependency-free Python file. On 5,000 held-out episodes, three off-policy estimators with different failure modes agree: the shipped policy is worth +15.25 per thread (DR +16.79, 95% CI 14.6 to 16.0), against −13.46 for the policy that produced the logs and +1.10 for sending nothing at all.

1Problem setting

Each episode is one follow-up thread of at most four decisions. At each step the policy sees the observation (topic, equipment, season, customer signal, touch and ignore counts, patience, and four noisy scores) and returns one of eight actions. PARK_THREAD and ESCALATE_TO_HUMAN end the episode. The logs record the old policy's action, its propensity π_β(a|s), the reward and the next state, and nothing about actions it did not take.

The reward is dominated by one rare, expensive outcome. Opt-outs happen on 7.9% of logged steps and cost −62.6 each, more than half the value of a sale.

2Method

2.1 Fitted Q-Iteration

We fit Q(s, a) by iterated regression on the training logs. Round one regresses the immediate reward; each later round regresses r + Qk(s′, argmaxa′ Qk(s′, a′)), and terminal steps bootstrap from zero. Episodes are at most four steps, so we use no discount and five rounds cover the full horizon with one to spare. The regressor is a histogram gradient-boosted tree with the action as a categorical input, which lets one model share structure across all eight actions.

2.2 Guardrails as relabelling, not post-processing

Some decisions should not be left to a model trained on thin data. An unhappy customer is the clearest case: Table 2 shows that every outbound action loses money there, and only a human handoff pays. We encode five such rules and apply them before distillation, so the tree learns the rule boundaries directly. The same rules run again at inference as a first layer (Algorithm 1), so they hold even where the tree is imperfect.

2.3 Distillation

The shipped artifact is a CART tree (depth 10, 148 leaves, balanced class weights, min 50 samples per leaf) trained to imitate the relabelled greedy policy on the 74,733 training states. It reproduces the teacher's action on 85.5% of them (weighted F1 0.87) and is exported as nested if/else statements. That removes the runtime dependency on scikit-learn and gives a reviewer a policy they can read line by line. Section 5 measures what the distillation costs.

3Evaluation protocol

With no simulator and no live traffic, each policy is scored on the 5,000 held-out episodes by three estimators. SNIPS reweights logged rewards by π(a|s)/π_β(a|s), clipped at 10 and self-normalised; it is model-free but only sees steps where the candidate agrees with the log, so it is reported per step. The direct method fits a fresh Fitted Q-Evaluation model for each candidate and reads its value at turn 0. Doubly robust starts from the FQE prediction and corrects it with importance-weighted residuals, and is consistent if either the model or the weights are right. When DM and DR agree, the two ways of being wrong have not both happened.

Before trusting any of this we score the old policy on its own logs, where the answer is known.

Two of these deserve a plain reading. On unhappy customers, both rule-based policies and ours land near +20 because the escalation guardrail makes the same decision in all three; the learned model contributes nothing there. On membership renewal, an early version of the learned policy scored −4.97 by over-messaging members. A targeted rule (touch once at turn 0, then wait) brought it to −1.29, still below doing nothing. 7What the policy does differently

Figure 5 compares action frequencies on the same held-out states. The logged policy spends most of its decisions on estimate nudges and objection questions. The learned policy cuts those sharply and moves the volume into waiting, parking stale threads, membership touches, and offering scheduling once a customer has signalled interest.

8Limitations

These results are offline estimates. They bound what a live test is likely to show; they do not replace one.

  1. No counterfactuals. The logs only record the old policy's choices. It almost never waited on turn 0 of an open estimate, so the value of doing so is extrapolated by the Q-model, not measured.
  2. Stitched trajectories. FQE chains one-step predictions into trajectories that may never occur in the data. always_wait scores +1.10, yet no logged episode waits at every step.
  3. Distribution shift. A policy that messages less changes how customers respond later in the thread. The logs describe customers who were messaged often.
  4. Thin support where errors are costly. Table 7 shows the two terminal actions have the least data, and the new policy uses both more than the old one did.

Rollout plan

Ship to 5% of traffic behind the existing policy. Track opt-out rate daily and roll back if it exceeds 1.2× the control for three consecutive days. Track escalation volume against CSR capacity, since the new policy escalates every unhappy customer, and roll back if it runs above 3× the offline prediction. Compare the live action mix with Figure 5 weekly; a large gap means the offline states no longer describe live traffic.
